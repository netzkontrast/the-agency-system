# The words: spoken and on screen

The writer owns every word in the film. Most weak films have too many.

## Before writing
- Write and count the spoken lines before the storyboard is timed (about two words a second, plus the gaps). The script sets the film's length, not the other way round.
- One takeaway, one sentence. Every line either sets it up or pays it off.
- Use the person's own words and the brand's real lines where they exist. A line you wrote is marked as yours when you show the script, and a made-up brand's copy is yours too.
- No claim, number, price, date or link they did not give.

## Spoken lines
- Each line must be clear to someone who hears it once with no context. Read it cold: if it needs the picture or the line before to make sense, rewrite it. Copy lifted from a web page rarely passes.
- Conversational, the way a person would say it to a friend. Short sentences. One idea each.
- Avoid words with two meanings and trade words ("music", not "score").
- Budget: about two words a second, less under fast cuts. Count the words against the length before recording, and cut lines, not pace.
- A line that repeats an earlier line has to earn it.

- **After an aside, name the subject again.** A pronoun attaches to the last thing said. "Oh, and there's a copywriter for social, too. It made that video." was heard by the owner as the copywriter making the video. "Take One made that video." cannot be misheard, costs two words, and puts the name in the viewer's ear once more. Read every line that starts with "it", "this" or "that" together with the line before it.
- **"That" for the thing shown, "this" for the film itself.** When a film points at a second film inside it, keep the two words apart: "that video" (the one in the player), then "the one you're watching". "This video" too early is heard as the whole film and gives the turn away.

## On-screen lines
- Two to five words at a time, one idea per shot, large.
- No full stop at the end of a line on screen.
- Plain words. If the same thing is spoken and shown, the screen carries the key words, not the whole sentence.
- Reading time and placement: `references/timing.md`. Never on a face.

- **A first line said large: build it in place as one stacked block, and let nothing fly.** Set the line as a left-aligned stack, one short line per spoken beat, the key word last and about two and a half times larger ("you built / something / big"). The first line is there on frame 0; each next line rises into place from behind its own lower edge (a wipe of about 0.15 s, eased out) exactly when it is said; the last, largest line rises the same way with a small overshoot (3 %; more touches the line above). The whole sentence is then on screen as a poster for the rest of the shot. With a cut-out of the subject, the stack sits behind it and the big word comes up from behind the product. What this replaced, and why: an earlier version showed each word large and then shrank it up to a small line while the next popped in. A reviewer passed it; the owner did not: "do you think the typography on the first frame is the best we can do ... the way it moves". Two things moving in different directions at once reads as a preset, and the small line cannot be read on a phone. One kind of move for the whole sentence, and words that stay where they land.

## The last line
- The name, one line, and one thing to do, with the link they gave from the first second of the end card. No link, no invented one: end on the name and tell the person a link is missing.
- A line that could sit under any product ("for everyone who...") is a placeholder. Find the one only this product can say.

## Words on screen when the film has a voice or a song

Many people watch muted. A film that only makes sense with the sound on has lost them.

- **Show short lines whole.** A spoken or sung line of up to about seven words goes on screen in full, broken into two or three big rows, with its key words lit. Fragments ("the work", "hand it off") look sharp to someone who knows the lyric and mean nothing to someone who does not.
- **Lead-in small, point large.** The row that sets up the line ("Not because") can be about 75 to 85 px at 1080p; the row that carries it ("the work goes away") is 100 px or more. Nothing a viewer must read is under about 70 px.
- **Each word arrives when it is said**, lit, then cools. Do not let a word sit half-lit and brown; it reads as low contrast.
- **Hold a finished line at least 1.2 s.** A line that completes and is covered or cut half a second later was never read.
- **Words get clean space.** Not on a grid of lines, not across an outline, not touching the frame edge, not on the thing they describe. Move the camera or the object, not the type size.
- **A repeated set says each phrase once, large, and then leaves it on its object** (the label lands on the card as the next phrase starts), so the set stays readable without saying anything twice.
- **Every stretch with a voice has words.** Three seconds of voice with nothing on screen is three seconds a muted viewer waits.
